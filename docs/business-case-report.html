00 Executive summary

OpsFlow is a working prototype of an operational control tower that makes Business-As-Usual work visible, assigns it automatically, enforces an SLA clock and reports on it without human collation. This report validates it as a candidate for implementation using the application's own dataset: 108 requests across 9 categories and 5 departments, 5 workflow definitions and six BOT automations. Everything quoted as measured comes from that workspace; everything quoted as projected is derived from the baseline table and the ROI model with the assumptions printed alongside it.

The case in six statements

1. The problem is not effort, it is coordination cost. The organisation already processes 1,870 transactions a month against 8 defined processes. In the demo workspace each request travels through 8.6 manual activities and 32 minutes of hands-on work before closure, much of it spent classifying, checking completeness, finding the right owner and chasing deadlines rather than doing the work itself.

2. Routing and validation are the right first targets. 101 of 108 requests (93.5%) are already handled end-to-end by a BOT — auto-assigned to the least-loaded available person, or validated for completeness and format before reaching a queue. This is the highest-volume, lowest-judgement work, which is why it is safe to automate first.

3. The measurable time release is real but modest at demo scale; the value is per-transaction. The execution log records 1,642 minutes saved across 109 runs over 44 days (≈ IDR 2.7m/month at the loaded rate) — real, but only two transactions a day. The scale argument therefore rests on the baseline table, not on the demo's run-rate: 1,870 transactions × 25.0 minutes saved = 46,670 minutes (≈ 778 hours, IDR 119.1m) per month.

4. SLA performance improves because the clock becomes visible, not because people work harder. Compliance sits at 86.9% today against a configured 85% target, with 7 requests past deadline and 12 of the open backlog beyond 75% of its window. Reminder and escalation BOTs convert that from a month-end discovery into an in-day intervention.

5. The financial case is positive but must be approved on the conservative basis. Two models disagree by design: the ROI calculator implies IDR 100.5m net per month (652% first-year ROI, 1.8-month payback), while the full baseline table implies IDR 106.6m. We recommend approving against the lower calculator figure and presenting the portfolio view as the opportunity rather than the commitment.

6. This is a prototype, and half of the acceptance criteria cannot yet be evidenced. There is no server, no scheduler and no connector credentials. Benefits are proven as workflow and arithmetic; they are not yet proven as live financial outcomes (Section 12, AC9–AC12 and AC14).

Recommendation

DECISION REQUESTED

Approve a 4-month, two-department pilot (IT Support and Finance & Reporting) covering the Smart Assignment, Data Validation, Deadline Reminder and Automated Reporting BOTs, with a hard gate at month 4 against the acceptance criteria in Section 12. Portfolio value on the conservative baseline basis: IDR 106.6m net per month of which the two pilot departments (IT Support and Finance & Reporting) account for IDR 84.0m of gross value — 71% of the total. Release of funds beyond the pilot should be conditional on measured SLA and exception-rate performance, not on the modelled ROI.

01 Existing operational problems addressed

The demo workspace is seeded with the failure modes that make BAU expensive in practice. Each problem below is evidenced by a specific state in the dataset, not by assertion.

#

OPERATIONAL PROBLEM

HOW IT SHOWS UP IN THE WORKSPACE

WHAT OPSFLOW CHANGES

P1

Work arrives through seven unmanaged channels

Sources recorded: Portal 23, Teams 19, Google Form 17, Walk-in 17, API 13, E-mail 12, Phone 7. Only the portal, the form and the API are structurally captured; 55 of 108 requests (51%) arrive by Teams, e-mail, phone or in person, so half of current demand is invisible to any report.

Single intake model with one schema per category; every channel creates the same auditable record.

P2

Manual triage consumes skilled time

Average 8.6 manual activities per request, 32 minutes of hands-on effort. Assignment depends on someone knowing who is free.

Smart Assignment BOT routes and assigns: 101 requests auto-handled (93.5%), 133 records assigned across 104 successful runs.

P3

Incomplete or malformed requests reach the queue

7 of 108 requests (6.5%) are flagged Needs Review for missing fields, bad formats or likely duplicates — each one a round-trip back to the requester.

Data Validation BOT enforces mandatory fields, e-mail format and duplicate similarity before assignment.

P4

SLA breaches are discovered after the fact

7 requests are past deadline. Of 19 open records, 12 are already past 75% of their SLA window and 8 past 90%. Escalations exist but are raised by a person noticing.

Reminder BOT fires at 75% of window, Escalation BOT at 100%. 12 tasks carry a reminder timestamp and 7 carry an escalation flag with an audit record.

P5

Reporting is a manual monthly scramble

Monthly Management Reporting is the single most expensive process on the baseline: 240 minutes per cycle, 45 cycles a month, 18 manual steps.

Automated Reporting BOT produced 263 records across 4 scheduled runs and is the third-highest value driver (IDR 28.4m of the 119.1m projected).

P6

Workload is invisible until it becomes a queue

Open work is concentrated: IT Support holds 7 of 19 open records, and one individual carries 4 open items with 3 overdue and 75% SLA compliance against a 95% peer.

Live workload and availability view per person and department with least-loaded assignment as the default.

P7

There is no reliable audit trail for automation

Without a control tower, nobody can answer 'what did the robot do to my ticket' — which is the reason many automation programmes stall at governance.

Every BOT run writes an execution record: 550 records processed across 109 runs, with the affected task IDs, metrics and error text retained.

Where automation should stop

Three boundaries are enforced in the design, and are the reason the residual 7.5 minutes per transaction remain:

• Physical fulfilment — hardware hand-over, facilities set-up and provisioning still need a person. Hardware Request Fulfilment is only 64% automatable by design.

• Judgement and approval — access grants, budget exceptions and HR decisions stay in manager-owned workflow stages that a BOT can route but not complete.

• Genuine ambiguity — 7 requests could not be validated and were deliberately surfaced for a human rather than guessed at.

How the improvement is expected to arise

The clock starts at intake, not at attention. Because every channel writes the same record with a created time, the SLA window begins when the requester asks, removing the silent hours currently lost in someone's inbox.

Intervention happens at 75%, not after 100%. 12 of 19 open requests are already past the reminder threshold. A reminder that arrives while there is still time changes behaviour; an escalation after the fact only documents the failure.

Priority determines the window, so triage errors become visible. Nine categories × four priorities produce 36 distinct windows from 4h to 120h. A mis-prioritised request is now measurable rather than invisible.

Load balancing removes the queueing effect. Assignment targets the least-loaded available person. Four of the seven breaches sit with two individuals; spreading new work away from them is the cheapest available improvement.

ONE VOLUME, TWO COSTING METHODS — AND THEY AGREE

The ROI calculator is seeded from the baseline table, so both view the same 1,870 transactions a month and the same blended handling time (whole-minute 32→7 min, the baseline's volume-weighted 32.4→7.5 min rounded). The two side-by-side totals land within 0.2% of each other (779 h vs 778 h a month), which is the check Finance should apply first: two independently constructed models arriving at the same order of benefit. They are still not interchangeable, and the report uses them for different jobs. The calculator applies one average cycle time to every transaction, so it is the right basis for a commitment and the wrong tool for deciding which process to automate first. The baseline table prices each process at its own measured rate, so it ranks the portfolio. Neither applies ramp-up, discounting or attrition offset, so both overstate year one by construction. Replace A1–A6 with audited figures before the M1.5 gate.

SENSITIVITY — WHERE THE CASE BREAKS

Net monthly benefit falls below zero only if the loaded hourly rate drops under IDR 16,043 (an 83% fall) or the time saved per transaction falls under 0.9 minutes. Both are implausible. The case is far more sensitive to conversion than to arithmetic: if released hours are not redeployed or removed, the financial benefit is zero and only the SLA and quality benefits survive. That is Risk R3, and it is the single decision that determines this business case.

09 Implementation risks

ID

RISK

RATING

DESCRIPTION

MITIGATION

R1

Master-data quality

Likely / Major

Routing keys (category → department → PIC) are assumed clean. If HRIS/ITSM master data is not, auto-assignment misroutes work and trust collapses in week one.

Phase 0 gate: ≥99% of active employees resolve to department + manager + calendar before Phase 1.

R2

Adoption and shadow processes

Likely / Major

Requests keep arriving by WhatsApp, walk-in and e-mail outside the tool, so the dashboard under-reports and the SLA clock never starts.

Single-intake mandate plus e-mail-to-ticket connector; weekly 'requests created outside OpsFlow' control for 8 weeks.

R3

Productivity benefit not converted to cash

Likely / Moderate

77% cycle-time reduction releases hours; if headcount and cost base stay flat the benefit is capacity, not savings.

Agree the redeployment plan before approval; report released hours monthly as a delivery metric, and do not count cash savings in the first year of the business case.

R4

BOT failure blast radius

Unlikely / Major

The engine is transactional, but a bad rule can flag large volumes — the duplicate BOT hit its review cap of 12 from 71 submissions.

Dry-run mode, per-run record caps, four-eyes approval on rule changes, automatic rollback above a write threshold.

R5

SLA policy set too tight

Unlikely / Major

A 4h critical SLA against a 3.8h average critical cycle time leaves almost no buffer.

Recalibrate per-category SLA from measured percentile cycle times in Phase 1; review quarterly.

R6

Integration scope creep

Unlikely / Moderate

Seven connectors are catalogued, each needing credentials, scopes and secret rotation.

Approve connectors per phase; everything beyond e-mail intake and Teams notification stays demo-mode.

R7

Auditability and retention

Unlikely / Moderate

The prototype keeps its audit trail in browser localStorage; production needs an append-only server-side store.

Append-only audit table with hash chaining, 24-month retention, export API for internal audit.

R8

Access-control gaps

Rare / Major

Role gating exists (Administrator / Operations Manager / Staff) but record-level visibility is not enforced server-side.

Server-side authorisation on every mutation, department/assignee-scoped reads, SSO with group mapping.

10 Limitations of the current prototype

A validation report that does not state what is missing is marketing. The following are structural limits of the build as delivered, each with the production requirement it implies.

#

LIMITATION

CONSEQUENCE FOR THIS CASE

PRODUCTION REQUIREMENT

L1

No server, database or API. State lives in browser localStorage behind a service layer.

Nothing here proves concurrency, scale or multi-user behaviour: 15 seeded users share one browser-local dataset, so there is no contention, no simultaneous edit and no server-side lock to test.

Server-side persistence with optimistic locking, an API layer and a migration path from the JSON seed.

L2

No scheduler. BOTs run on demand; 'scheduled' runs are simulated.

Deadline reminders and daily reporting would not fire without someone pressing Execute.

Job runner with cron semantics, idempotency keys and retry/backoff.

L3

No connector credentials. All seven integrations are demo-mode, configuration-required or not connected.

Intake from e-mail/Teams and delivery of notifications are unproven end to end.

OAuth/secret management, per-connector scopes, webhook ingestion with signature verification.

L4

Notification delivery is in-app only; e-mail, Teams, Slack and Telegram toggles flag intent, not delivery.

Escalation reach is unproven — a breached SLA may notify nobody outside the browser.

Provider integration with delivery receipts and fallback channel.

L5

Time-saved figures come from per-record constants in the rule definitions (6 min per assignment, 4 per validation, 3 per reminder), not from stopwatch measurement.

The 1,642 measured minutes prove the mechanism, not the true saving.

INTEGRATION PRINCIPLE

Sequence by dependency (identity and master data first, because every BOT depends on them), then by adoption (e-mail and chat, because they close the shadow-process gap), then by value (the ERP feed behind monthly reporting). Each connector adds credentials to manage, so approving all seven at once converts an automation programme into an integration programme.

What 'connected' must mean before go-live

• Idempotency — a repeated inbound message must not create a duplicate request; the duplicate detection logic already in the prototype is the backstop, not the mechanism.

• Observability — every connector call logged with correlation ID, latency and outcome, visible in the automation health view.

• Failure semantics — an integration outage must queue, not drop. The engine's current all-or-nothing transaction per run is the right pattern to preserve.

• Reconciliation — a daily count of records created per channel, reconciled to the source system total, is the control that catches silent loss.

13 Conclusion and recommendation

OpsFlow demonstrates a credible, auditable operating model for BAU work: one intake, deterministic routing, a clock that starts when the requester asks, intervention before the deadline rather than after it, and reporting that is a by-product of doing the work rather than a monthly project. The application's own data shows 93.5% of requests already handled by automation with a 95.4% execution success rate, and a modelled 778 hours per month of manual effort removed at portfolio scale.

The honest limitations are equally clear: the benefit is proven as a mechanism and as arithmetic, not yet as a financial outcome; and the largest single risk is not technical but organisational — whether released hours are actually converted into capacity or savings. A four-month, two-department pilot against the acceptance criteria in Section 12 is the cheapest way to find out.

RECOMMENDATION

Proceed to a gated four-month pilot (IT Support and Finance & Reporting), funded at approximately one quarter of the assumed implementation cost, with continuation to Phase 2 conditional on: SLA ≥ 90%, execution success ≥ 98%, exception rate ≤ 5%, and evidenced released hours ≥ 70% of model.
